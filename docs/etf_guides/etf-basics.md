# ETF Guides

## What is an ETF?
An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a basket of securities, such as stocks or bonds, that trades on an exchange like a single stock. ETFs give investors instant diversification across many holdings in one purchase, without needing to buy each underlying security individually.

## ETFs vs Mutual Funds
Both ETFs and mutual funds pool money from many investors into a diversified basket of securities. The key difference is that ETFs trade throughout the day at fluctuating prices like a stock, while mutual funds are priced and traded only once per day after market close. ETFs also typically have lower expense ratios and are generally more tax-efficient than actively managed mutual funds.

## Index ETFs
An index ETF is designed to track a specific market index, such as the S&P 500, as closely as possible, rather than trying to outperform it. Index ETFs are popular because of their low cost, broad diversification, and strong historical track record relative to actively managed alternatives over the long term.

## Sector and Thematic ETFs
Sector ETFs focus on a single industry, such as technology or healthcare, while thematic ETFs focus on a specific trend, such as clean energy or artificial intelligence. These offer more targeted exposure than a broad index ETF but carry higher concentration risk since they are not diversified across the whole market.

## Bond ETFs
Bond ETFs hold a basket of fixed income securities, giving investors diversified bond exposure without needing to buy individual bonds, which can require large minimum investments. Bond ETFs are commonly used to fill the fixed income portion of a diversified portfolio.

## Expense Ratios
The expense ratio is the annual fee an ETF charges, expressed as a percentage of assets invested, and is deducted automatically from the fund's returns. Even small differences in expense ratio compound significantly over long time horizons, so cost is an important factor when comparing similar ETFs.
