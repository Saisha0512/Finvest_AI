# Rebalancing

## What is Rebalancing?
Rebalancing is the process of periodically buying or selling assets in a portfolio to restore it to its original or target asset allocation. Over time, some assets grow faster than others, causing the portfolio to drift away from its intended risk level.

## Why Rebalancing Matters
If equities perform well for several years, a portfolio originally set at 60% stocks and 40% bonds might drift to 75% stocks and 25% bonds. This drifted portfolio is now riskier than the investor originally intended, even though no explicit decision was made to take on more risk.

## Threshold-Based Rebalancing
One common approach is to rebalance whenever an asset class drifts more than a set threshold from its target, commonly 5 percentage points. For example, if the bond target is 40% and bonds fall to 34% or below, or rise above 46%, it triggers a rebalance back to the 40% target.

## Calendar-Based Rebalancing
Another approach is to rebalance on a fixed schedule, such as quarterly or annually, regardless of how far the portfolio has drifted. This is simpler to implement and communicate to users than threshold-based rebalancing, though it can be less responsive to large, sudden market moves.

## Tax Considerations When Rebalancing
Selling appreciated investments in a taxable account to rebalance can trigger capital gains taxes. Where possible, rebalancing should first be attempted by directing new contributions toward underweighted asset classes, and by rebalancing within tax-advantaged accounts, before selling appreciated positions in a taxable account.

## Rebalancing and Emotional Discipline
Rebalancing forces a disciplined "sell high, buy low" behavior: trimming asset classes that have grown and are now overweighted, and adding to asset classes that have lagged and are now underweighted. This runs counter to natural investor instinct, which is why a systematic process or automated reminder is valuable.
